The **Togo example is particularly strong** because it explains three concepts simultaneously without artificial examples:

> **78%** → purpose-restricted road maintenance  
> **10%** → unrestricted Treasury  
> **12%** → cost recovery

Therefore the instrument is a **partial earmark**, not “90% earmarked,” and the 12% cost-recovery component is conceptually separate. That is exactly the kind of distinction your taxonomy was designed to preserve. Your taxonomy explicitly requires cost-recovery channels to be set aside before rolling the remaining proceeds channels up to E1/E2/E3 status. 

And the CIV tobacco example beautifully demonstrates why **instrument count ≠ allocation count**: one revenue source creates four earmarked channels, yet it remains one earmarked instrument for prevalence. That is the exact reason your methodology separately identifies `instrument_id` and `pair_id`.

## And the South Africa example is better than a generic “validation” story

You said you don't remember a formal special Chad/CIV validation exercise. That's fine. **We should not invent one.**

Instead, slide 14 can eventually show what rigorous validation actually means in practice:

> **Policy description:** environmental levy supports recycling/waste policy  
> **Legal evidence:** proceeds paid into the National Revenue Fund  
> **Database decision:** **not coded as a statutory earmark**

That demonstrates an extremely important property of the method:

> **The model is not simply looking for an apparent tax–policy association; it requires an enacting source-to-destination assignment.**

That is far more persuasive than saying “I manually checked two countries and it seemed accurate.”

It also directly illustrates your operative rule that a policy objective, fund name, explanatory statement or appropriation is not enough; the assignment itself must be legally enacted. 

So I would consider the previous idea of a “Chad/CIV validation exercise” dropped unless you later find concrete validation statistics.
