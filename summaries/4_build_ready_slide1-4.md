## 1. Final title

### Bottom empirical hook

> **77%** of respondents across **34 African countries** say it is difficult to find out how government uses tax revenues.

That is the exact defensible number from Afrobarometer's 2019/21 surveys. ([Afrobarometer][2])

---

## 3. Why these claims are supported

### Political acceptability

Flores-Macías' randomized survey experiment in Mexico tests institutional design features including **earmarking** and finds that these features increase political support for taxation, particularly among low-trust respondents. The outcome is **support**, not actual tax compliance. ([IDEAS/RePEc][3])

Prichard's Ghana case also provides direct process evidence: he describes the GET Fund as first and foremost a politically motivated means of securing passage of the VAT increase. ([The Crucible Institute][4])

So:

> **“may make a tax easier to support”**

is supported.

Do **not** write:

> “earmarking increases compliance.”

That is not established.

### Rigidity and fungibility

Cashin et al.'s health-earmarking work explicitly describes longer-run offsets through fungibility and warns that rigidities can increase over time. ([WHO IRIS][5])

### Budget fragmentation

Allen and Radev explicitly discuss loss of aggregate expenditure control, distorted allocation, reduced transparency, cash-management problems and the potential for proliferation of separate funds to fragment the overall PFM system. ([eLibrary IMF][6])

So the right-hand side is firmly grounded.

---

## 3. What exactly is literature-supported here?

The **chain itself is your synthesis**, not a sequence proposed verbatim by one paper. Your literature review explicitly arrives at a sequence from de jure design through political authorization, collection, budget translation and expenditure performance. 

Each stage, however, has direct literature support.

### Political authorization

Prichard documents a political-passage role for Ghana's GET Fund earmark. ([The Crucible Institute][4])

Flores-Macías experimentally finds increased political support from institutional tax-design features including earmarking. ([IDEAS/RePEc][3])

### Revenue realization

Bird and Jun explicitly caution that the final effect on compliance and revenue is **not obvious** and that more earmarking need not imply more taxes. ([International Center for Public Policy][7])

This is why the stage says:

> **“Does it affect collection and the tax base?”**

not:

> “Earmarking increases collection.”

### Budget translation

Bird and Jun distinguish nominal assignment from whether earmarked revenue actually becomes the **marginal source** of expenditure and discuss fungibility and offsets. ([International Center for Public Policy][7])

Cashin et al. similarly find that offsets and fungibility can erode the spending effect of an earmark. ([WHO IRIS][5])

### Expenditure outcomes / institutional conditions

Allen and Radev emphasize that the performance and risks of dedicated funds depend strongly on governance, financial management, audit, budget integration and administrative capacity. ([IMF][8])

So the mechanism chain is defensible, provided it remains labeled as your synthesis.

## 9. Oral explanation

Approximately **60 seconds**:

> “The literature suggests that we should not think about earmarking as having one single effect. First, there is the legal design itself: what exactly does the statute commit? That design may affect political authorization—whether a tax can be adopted or sustained. But political support does not automatically imply more revenue. The next question is whether collection or the tax base actually changes. Even if revenue is raised, a third distinction is whether the dedicated proceeds are actually transferred and whether they generate additional spending rather than substitute for general revenue. Finally, additional resources still have to be converted into effective expenditure and services.”

Then:

> “And those links depend on credibility, public financial management, governance and implementation capacity.”

Then the key sentence:

> **“Once we separate those stages, the empirical evidence turns out to be much less conclusive than a simple pro-versus-con debate suggests.”**

That opens slide 5 perfectly.
