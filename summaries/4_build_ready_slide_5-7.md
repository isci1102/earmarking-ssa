## 3. Why each row is defensible

### Political authorization — **some evidence**

This is the strongest positive part of the empirical literature, but the wording needs to remain narrow.

Flores-Macías uses a randomized survey experiment in Mexico. Tax-design provisions including earmarking, oversight and sunset clauses increased **stated political support**; the paper does not measure payments, compliance or revenue. ([ScienceDirect][1])

For SSA, Prichard provides direct Ghana case/process evidence. He writes that creation of the GET Fund was primarily a politically motivated means of securing passage of the VAT increase. ([The Crucible Institute][2])

Therefore:

> ✅ **Can raise stated support or facilitate passage in specific settings**

Not:

> ❌ “Earmarking increases tax acceptance.”

And not:

> ❌ “Earmarking causes higher revenue.”

---

### Compliance & net revenue — **unresolved**

Bird and Jun explicitly discuss opposite possible compliance mechanisms and conclude that the final outcome is not obvious; greater earmarking need not imply greater taxation. ([ResearchGate][3])

Flores-Macías observes political support, not tax liability, payment or collection. Your literature review likewise finds no study identifying the causal effect of earmarking on aggregate net tax revenue in SSA. 

Therefore:

> ✅ **Sign and magnitude remain unresolved**

This is stronger academically than saying merely “evidence is limited.”

---

### Additional spending — **mixed**

Bird and Jun review highly mixed empirical evidence. In some road cases, earmarking increased spending only by a fraction of dedicated revenues because general-fund support fell; in education cases, results range from no effect to reductions to increases. They explicitly emphasize revenue fungibility. ([ResearchGate][3])

Cashin, Sparkes and Bloom reach the same caution from health-earmarking cases. They state that it is impossible to know the no-earmark counterfactual in their six cases, that budgets are fungible, and that dedicated revenues can be offset by reductions elsewhere. 

Therefore:

> ✅ **Fungibility and general-fund offsets can weaken additionality**

I prefer **“can weaken”** to “often weaken,” because it makes fewer quantitative claims than the evidence supports.

---

### Expenditure quality / PFM — **conditional**

Cashin et al. stress PFM capacity, absorptive capacity and implementation constraints; their cross-country evidence does not establish a generic performance effect of earmarking. 

Allen and Radev similarly argue that extrabudgetary arrangements can undermine expenditure control, prioritization, transparency and cash management, while benefits are possible under strong governance and financial-management systems. ([eLibrary IMF][4])

Therefore:

> ✅ **Effects depend strongly on governance and implementation capacity**

## 4. Verification of the standard-data claim

This one can be made very precisely.

The OECD/AUC/ATAF *Revenue Statistics in Africa 2025* interpretative guide states:

> “Earmarking of a tax for specific purposes does not affect the classification of tax receipts.”

That is §29 of the tax-classification guide. ([OECD][5])

The report also explains that public revenues are primarily classified according to the taxable base. ([OECD][6])

So our presentation inference is:

> **Standard revenue statistics can tell us what type of tax was collected, but their classification does not encode the statutory destination created by earmarking.**

That is accurate.
